# Manuka Honey as a Skincare Ingredient: Composition, Function, and Formulation Uses

Manuka honey (a concentrated botanical extract harvested from the *Leptospermum scoparium* shrub of New Zealand and Australia) is one of the few natural substances with documented antibacterial activity, skin barrier support, and wound-care relevance in a single ingredient. Manuka honey in skincare formulas delivers methylglyoxal, manuka honey responsible for its antibacterial potency, directly to the skin surface, where it disrupts bacterial biofilm formation — a mechanism confirmed in laboratory findings on *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Maddocks et al., Microbiology, 2012). The manuka honey skincare ingredient belongs to a narrow class of actives that address multiple skin concerns simultaneously: acne, atopic dermatitis, dry skin, and compromised barrier function.

*Leptospermum scoparium* honey draws clinical attention specifically because standard-grade honeys lack comparable methylglyoxal concentrations. Harrison's case study published in *Plastic Surgical Nursing* (2017) documented its use on a severe atopic contact dermatitis eczematous reaction, providing direct evidence for its role in conditions resistant to conventional approaches. Skin with eczema, chronic dryness, or post-inflammatory sensitivity responds to manuka honey's combination of moisture-drawing ingredient (humectant) draw and antimicrobial surface action.

What manuka honey does for skin extends beyond moisture delivery. The sugar matrix pulls water into the upper skin layers, the low water activity environment inhibits bacterial growth, and medical-grade formulations address wound-adjacent properties in peristomal skin complication protocols (White et al., British Journal of Nursing, 2019). ## What is Manuka Honey in Skincare?

Manuka honey in skincare is a bioactive floral honey sourced from bees that feed on the *Leptospermum scoparium* (tea tree) shrub native to New Zealand and southeastern Australia. Manuka honey composition sets it apart from conventional honey: it contains elevated concentrations of methylglyoxal (MGO), a compound responsible for its potent antimicrobial activity, alongside hydrogen peroxide, defensin-1, and a broad spectrum of phenolic acids that support skin barrier function.

Manuka honey's high osmolarity draws moisture into the skin while the low water activity within the honey creates an environment hostile to bacterial growth. Manuka Honey's two properties work simultaneously — the honey hydrates compromised skin and limits the microbial load that disrupts barrier repair. Wounds treated with manuka honey showed faster reepithelialization compared to control wounds in a porcine burn model, supporting its use in skin-recovery contexts (Privrodski B et al., *Pharmaceuticals (Basel, Switzerland)*, 2025).

The MGO in manuka honey is manuka honey that distinguishes it from standard floral honeys, which rely almost entirely on hydrogen peroxide for antimicrobial effect. MGO forms through the non-enzymatic conversion of dihydroxyacetone, a compound uniquely concentrated in *Leptospermum scoparium* nectar. Sixty percent of participants in a face serum trial showed improvements in skin brightness following manuka honey use (O'Gorman E et al., *Cureus*, 2025). For consumers researching [moisture-drawing skincare ingredients](/ingredients) alongside manuka honey, the *Leptospermum scoparium* honey standardized under the INCI nomenclature "Leptospermum scoparium Mel" is the regulated form found in cosmetic formulations.

## How Does Manuka Honey Work on Skin?

Manuka honey works on skin through three simultaneous mechanisms: moisture-drawing ingredient (humectant) water-binding, sustained antibacterial activity from methylglyoxal, and enzymatic hydrogen peroxide release. The manuka honey mechanism begins at the surface — the high sugar concentration draws atmospheric moisture directly into the outer skin layer (stratum corneum), making manuka honey a moisture-drawing ingredient (humectant) that rivals synthetic alternatives in water retention. Manuka honey is harvested exclusively from the *Leptospermum scoparium* shrub native to New Zealand and Australia, and its bioactive profile differs from generic honeys because of the elevated methylglyoxal content specific to that plant source.

How manuka honey works diverges from standard honey at the cellular level. Methylglyoxal disrupts bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to microbial proteins, creating an environment where opportunistic bacteria struggle to colonize damaged skin. Manuka honey's acidity, combined with its osmotic draw, further desiccates the surface environment bacteria need to replicate, a dual-action effect that generic floral honeys do not replicate at the same magnitude.

Wound-care literature documents manuka honey's surface effects across multiple controlled observations. Manuka honey produced a significantly greater reduction of wound surface area compared to standard treatment in a leg ulcer debridement trial (Doerler et al., Journal of the German Society of Dermatology, 2012). A 25-patient postoperative neurosurgical wound study prescribed topical active leptospermum honey without patient-related adverse events across all participants (Felbaum et al., Advances in Skin & Wound Care, 2021). White, writing in the Journal of Wound Care (2016), concluded that manuka honey appears especially effective among honey varieties, distinguishing it from the generic honeys that dominated earlier wound-care inquiry.

The manuka honey moisture-drawing ingredient (humectant) property matters in daily skincare because dehydrated skin accelerates barrier breakdown and surface cracking. Yes, manuka honey is safe for topical use across most skin types. Dry, sensitive, acne-prone, and eczema-prone skin all respond well to its moisture-drawing ingredient (humectant) and antibacterial properties.

Manuka honey skin types span a wide range because methylglyoxal targets surface bacteria without disrupting the skin barrier the way synthetic actives do.
